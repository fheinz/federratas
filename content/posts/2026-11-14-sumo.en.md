The other day I wrote about how [Shinto worship is essentially
individual and self-service]({{< ref "2026-11-01-shinto.md" >}}).

To absolutely no-one's surprise, of course, I botched it.

On the one hand, there are the *matsuri*: seasonal festivals
celebrated in various ways across different regions, marking the
rhythm of rural life governed by the lunar calendar.

They take the most varied forms: lantern releases, parades of
illuminated boats on the river, small flotillas of fishing boats
making pilgrimages around certain islands. There are processions with
floats that remind me of Catholic feast days, except here the floats
can be five meters high and weigh a ton. And it’s less of a procession
and more of [a race where teams of men in
loincloths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hakata_Gion_Yamakasa)
compete to see who can shoulder their float across a five-kilometer
course in the shortest time.

What might be surprising is that the world-famous sumo wrestlers are
also officiating a religious ceremony in their loincloths (let’s not
dwell too much on the loincloth thing though: at least they're wearing
*something*. There was no such thing at the Greek Olympics).

Japanese culture is very heavy on ritual, and sumo is another one of
those. The bout itself lasts mere seconds and is the only spontaneous
part.  The rest of the time is devoted to a highly detailed,
repetitive choreography.

First, an officiant in traditional garb, white fan in hand, chants —
he does not announce, he *chants* — the names and regions of origin of
the contenders. He elongates the syllables with a musicality entirely
alien to Western sensibilities.

The wrestlers then enter the circle and face off in a squat. They clap
and rub their hands, arms extended in front of their chests, following
a strictly choreographed sequence. Then comes the *shiko*:[^1]
balancing on one leg, they lift the other and bring it down in a stomp
intended to purify the ground and drive evil spirits away. Then they
repeat the process with the other leg.

The display of balance is staggering. Their massive frames, supported
by a single muscular pillar, the other leg suspended in mid-air. It
looks impossible. Some hold the pose for several seconds, leg high
above their head, before finally slowly coming down.

In Shinto, everything revolves around purification. Having stomped the
ground clean, each wrestler retreats to his corner. He rinses his
mouth with a ladle of water (which he then spits out); he wipes away
sweat with a white paper towel, and finally grabs a handful of salt
(salt, like water, purifies) to scatter over the circle as he returns,
purifying it further still.

The wrestlers then face off in a squat again. The bout only begins
once both plant both fists on the ground, but this rarely happens right
away.

One of them might plant both fists on the ground while the other may
lower only one and hold off on the other&hellip; but before he gets to
it, the first wrestler might stand up and retreat to his corner. The
other one follows suit, and they repeat the *entire* purification
ritual. All of it. This can repeat over and over for up to four
minutes. The crowd’s reaction is interesting: as the number of false
starts increases, the applause when a wrestler stands back up gets
louder. I’m not quite sure which one of the wrestlers they’re cheering
for.

Finally the bout, which is over almost before it starts. For a few
seconds, they move with improbable agility for men of such bulk, until
one of them is shoved out of the circle or touches the clay with
anything other than the soles of his feet.

The ritual doesn't end there. A full day features twenty-one
bouts. Midway through, two *yokozuna* (grand champions) do a small
performance, one after the other. Besides the requisite stomping, they
[shuffle across the circle in a squat, using only their heels and
toes](https://youtu.be/CO_J_h2G8jI?si=Us_UcUgEAJn4q940&t=68). It looks
like something out of a cartoon and elicits roars from the crowd. At
the day's end, another *yokozuna* performs a similar rite, this time
wielding a bow.

It is a curious spectacle. One leaves deeply, repeatedly, and
thoroughly purified. Come to think of it, what strikes me most is
that, despite the modern struggle to keep pews filled, no Western
religion has yet had the foresight to declare football a sacrament.
