Following the [acquisition of Palm by
Hewlett-Packard](http://technologizer.com/2010/04/28/hp-buys-palm/),
the site [Technologizer](http://technologizer.com/) published an
article showcasing [patents that illustrate the ideas Palm explored
throughout its
history](http://technologizer.com/2010/04/29/palm-patents/). The
article is presented in a very irritating fashion (you must visit 11
different pages to read it in full), but it's reasonably
entertaining.  Its greatest value, however, lies in showing a couple
of examples of how patents are granted before the invention itself
even exists.

The first example is a portable videoconferencing device. It would
allow people to communicate using audio, video, and shared documents
via a wireless broadband connection. There is no doubt it would be a
useful device. The problem is that no one today, not even Palm, knows
how to build a device with those specifications.

A patent is not supposed to cover the "what," but the "how." One does
not patent a "horseless carriage," but rather a specific way of
mounting an internal combustion engine onto a chassis to drive the
wheels. The patent description should be such that a person skilled in
the art could, simply by following the instructions, obtain the
desired result.  In this case, however, Palm patented the concept
without yet knowing how to build it. Consequently, if processing,
communications, and software eventually reach the level required to
produce this, the actual inventors will have to solicit a license from
Hewlett-Packard to market *their own* invention.

Another example from the article is this narrow phone. When opened, it
reveals a standard-width electronic organizer thanks to a folding
screen. It is a pity that, save for a few prototypes that only
function under laboratory conditions, on Tuesdays during banking
hours, at low tide, and only if the user is free of dandruff, folding
screens remain with both feet firmly planted in the realm of science
fiction.

Again, the day someone actually invents a folding screen that makes
this design practical, they will be unable to use it in products
unless Hewlett-Packard grants them permission.

Whatever happened to the idea that patents were meant to favor
inventors?
