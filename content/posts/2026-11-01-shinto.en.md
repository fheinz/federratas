title: 'A Self-Service Religion'
---

The faithful first purify their hands and mouth at a fountain, then
queue before the shrine. They do not enter; the shrine is
vacant. Inside, only a few artifacts that harbor the deity (the
Kami). And that's assuming there is anything at all. Or even a
physical shrine: no one finds it strange to be told "there lies the
shrine" only to find nothing more thanb a clearing in the forest
clearing and the one element that is never absent: the offering box.

It's a wooden box the pilgrim encounters when their turn in line
arrives. Prayer consists of tossing a small coin into the box,
clapping twice in front of the chest, bowing once, and clapping once
more. In many shrines, a thick rope hangs from the ceiling. Shaking it
rings a bell. It does not chime like a bell, however. It sounds more
like a hoarse maraca.

At some point in the process, which I imagine between the bow and the
final clap, comes the petition: success in business, in love, good
grades at school, peace somewhere in the world (it's not as if there
isn't plenty to choose from).

These shrines are everywhere. In any neighborhood street, one finds
minuscule shrines, two meters by two, or grand shrines, even complexes
with multiple identical structures on the same grounds.

The Kami themselves are an intriguing concept. They are not creator gods
of nature, but spirits emerging from it. A waterfall has its Kami. So
does a particularly large or ancient tree, or a stone of peculiar shape.
Some have names, others do not. They are neither omnipotent nor
omniscient, though they possess powers whose scope remains somewhat
obscure to me.

The untrained eye manages to distinguish two types of shrine. Those
dedicated to Inari (a Kami so popular he has secured a place in the
hearts of Buddhists as well) are recognizable by the paths demarcated
with orange-painted wooden portals (torii), and by a particular
aesthetic featuring statues of Inari's messenger foxes adorned with
red bibs.  The remaining shrines are wooden edifices with no special
characteristics to distinguish one from another.

I find it noteworthy that it seems I'm not the only one confused by
the specific role of each shrine. A friend stopped to pray before one
of the shrines in a complex containing at least thirty. When he
finished, he told me he had prayed for peace in Gaza. I asked which
Kami he had addressed. He looked at me with surprise, as if the
question had never occurred to him. The answer was "I don't know," and
we ended up laughing at how disoriented the Kami of Crooked-Tailed
Cats must have been, wondering what on earth a "Gaza" is.

Meanwhile, there is no priest, no monk, nor anything of the sort in
sight. No one to hear confession (hardly necessary, as there is no sin)
or celebrate mass. The ritual is individual, not collective, and rests
entirely upon the practitioner. I imagine the clergy's role is limited
to the maintenance and cleaning of the shrines (invariably pristine) and
the regular emptying of the offering box.

Religion informs society, and I am struck by the parallel between
Shinto ritual and the vending machines that are ubiquitous in Japan,
which dispense a vast array of products. There is a fascination with
automation and self-service that leads, for instance, to chain
restaurants having screens at every table for orders delivered by
robots or conveyor belts.  Ultimately, it appears they invented the
automated, self-service cult centuries ago.
