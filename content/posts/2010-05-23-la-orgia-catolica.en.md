---
title: "The Catholic Orgy"
date: 2010-05-23
categories: ["Atentados paraliterarios", "Orgías"]
---

Many scholars say that Christianity's main contribution to human
thought was the idea of loving one's neighbor. They also claim it was
this what motivated another innovation: the aim of uniting the human
race in an “universal assembly,” aka “Catholic Church.”

Up until then, religions mostly concerned themselves with the imagery
of their own pantheons, without worrying too much about the gods of
others except maybe to plagiarize one that looked particularly
well-crafted.

The new expansionist ideal of Catholicism, however, required the
conversion of everyone to a single, specific God, which motivated
countless evangelizing campaigns through the centuries and may have
planted the seed of what we now call marketing.

The first obstacle evangelists faced was how much Romans resisted
conversion to the new faith. A very understandable reluctance, of
course: from meetings in gloomy catacombs to the occasional fifteen
minutes of fame as an appetizer for the lions at the Circus Maximus,
the life of a believer in those times abounded in discomforts.

Far more serious, however, were the rather obvious personality
disorders of Yahweh, so foreign to the Roman psyche. They could cope
with the idea of him fathering a son with a mortal (their own
tradition was full of precedents) but the rest of his attitudes were
too contradictory.

Earthly misfortunes, which they had always interpreted as the snubs of
their own lascivious and irascible deities, were now to be understood,
somehow, as signs of the love of a mysterious and contemplative God,
who moreover preached “love one another” with the same nonchalance
with which he proclaimed celibacy as the ideal state.

It was likely this penchant for celibacy, so foreign to Roman
inclinations, that proved the most insurmountable when deciding on a
change of faith. Romans just couldn't get used to it, even converted
ones missed their old ways: regardless of the success of the
evangelizing campaigns, the desertion from the Lord's ranks during
bacchanal season was catastrophic.

Overwhelmed by these difficulties, Andrés de Limuria, one of the most
prominent evangelists of the time, dedicated himself to the study of
the Scriptures to determine to what extent orgiastic activity was
allowed by the Church. He began his research in secret, as he had
little hope of finding anything.

To his own surprise, however, he concluded that at least the most
conspicuous orgy-related behavior (spontaneous sexual union) could be
practiced without entering into conflict with Catholic laws. This
resulted from a punctilious application of dogma: according to
tradition, a religious marriage can be celebrated without the presence
of priests, as it is the contracting parties themselves who officiate
the ceremony. The priest is there merely as an optional witness. In
consequence, just by initiating a sexual relationship, the
participants could be understood to be creating the matrimonial bond
that simultaneously blessed it.

There was a glaring missing piece though: how to keep the orgy going
since, after that first copulation, the newlywed were disqualified
from changing partners. Nonetheless, this first triumph encouraged
Andrés to report his studies to the ecclesiastical authorities, while
inquiring into the implications of the formula “until death do us
part” in an orgiastic context.

He was never able to finish his studies: his superiors failed to grasp
the pious intention driving him, and ordered him to stop immediately.

For centuries, Andrés's writings were preserved and copied by
generations of Benedictine monks (some of whom took to reading them in
bed). It wasn't until the Middle Ages, of all times, that Andrés's
train of thought would be carried to its logical conclusion by an
obscure Spanish bishop: the orgy could go on if one of the spouses
died, leaving the survivor free to join another orgiast to celebrate a
new union without offending dogma.

The resulting orgy still had a problem of rhythm: it was too slow if
one had to wait for the natural arrival of the Reaper, while the
commandment “thou shalt not kill” placed clear limits on what could be
done about it.

Divine inspiration, however, was not long in coming: it was not lawful
to kill people, but the laws were different when it came to demons,
witches, or heretics. In these cases, the pious thing to do was in
fact to put them to death as thoroughly and painfully as
possible.

This made a Catholic orgy possible in the form of a series of
spontaneous, brief marriages, during which one of the spouses
discovered and denounced the demonic vocation of their partner, so
that the Church itself would execute them.

This mechanism not only eliminated all theological objections to the
sexual orgy in the Catholic context, it even provided a plenty of
opportunities for sado-masochism and ample excuses for spectacular
bonfires.

Thus it was that, after centuries of stagnation, the first and only
Catholic orgy recorded in History could be celebrated. It lasted for
decades, and even today we remember it by the name of The Holy
Inquisition.
